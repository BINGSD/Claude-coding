Statistics

Statistics questions reward clear interpretation as much as calculation. Always give conclusions in the context of the question, and use your calculator's distribution functions to save time.

12Sampling

Year 1
Key points
  • The population is everyone or everything of interest. A census measures the whole population; a sample measures part of it.
  • The sampling frame is the list of members the sample is chosen from.
  • Random methods: simple random (every sample equally likely), systematic (every kth item after a random start), stratified (sample each group in proportion to its size).
  • Non-random methods: quota (the interviewer fills set numbers from each group) and opportunity (whoever is available). Cheap and quick, but likely to be biased.
Formulas
  • Stratified sample sizefrom a stratum=stratum sizepopulation size×sample size
  • Systematic intervalk = population size ÷ sample size
ExampleA sixth form has 120 students in Year 12 and 80 in Year 13. Describe how to take a stratified sample of 30.
Show solutionHide solution
  1. Total population is 200. Year 12: 120/200 × 30 = 18. Year 13: 80/200 × 30 = 12.
  2. Number the students in each year group separately (Year 12: 1–120, Year 13: 1–80).
  3. Use a random number generator to choose 18 different numbers from Year 12 and 12 from Year 13.
18 from Year 12 and 12 from Year 13, chosen at random within each group
Exercise
  1. [2]Give one advantage and one disadvantage of opportunity sampling.
    Show answer

    Advantage: quick, easy and cheap. Disadvantage: unlikely to be representative of the population.

  2. [2]A researcher picks every 10th name from a register of 500 students, starting from a random number between 1 and 10. Name the method and state the sample size.
    Show answer

    Systematic sampling; sample size 50.

Common mistake

Quota sampling looks like stratified sampling because both use groups, but quota is not random. Stratified needs a sampling frame; quota does not.

13Data presentation & interpretation

Year 1
Key points
  • Measures of location: mean, median, mode. Measures of spread: range, interquartile range (IQR), standard deviation.
  • In a histogram, the area of each bar is proportional to frequency, so the height is frequency density.
  • Use linear interpolation to estimate the median and quartiles from grouped data.
  • Coding: if y = (x − a)/b, then x̄ = bȳ + a and σx = bσy. Adding a constant doesn't change the spread.
  • The PMCC r measures linear correlation, from −1 to 1. Correlation does not imply causation. Only use a regression line to predict within the range of the data (interpolation).
Formulas
  • Meanx‾=Σxn
  • Varianceσ2=Σx2n−x‾2
  • Outlier (common rule)below Q1 − 1.5 × IQR or above Q3 + 1.5 × IQR
  • Frequency densityfrequency ÷ class width
ExampleFind the mean and standard deviation of 4, 7, 8, 10, 11.
Show solutionHide solution
  1. Σx = 40, so x̄ = 40/5 = 8.
  2. Σx² = 16 + 49 + 64 + 100 + 121 = 350.
  3. σ² = 350/5 − 8² = 70 − 64 = 6, so σ = √6.
Mean 8, standard deviation 2.45 (3 s.f.)
Exercise
  1. [2]A data set has Q1 = 22 and Q3 = 34. Using the 1.5 × IQR rule, is 55 an outlier?
    Show answer

    Yes. The upper limit is 52, and 55 > 52.

    IQR = 12, 1.5 × 12 = 18, 34 + 18 = 52. (Lower limit 22 − 18 = 4.)

  2. [1]A histogram class 10–15 has frequency 30. Find the height of its bar.
    Show answer

    Frequency density 6

    30 ÷ 5 = 6.

Common mistake

Histogram questions often give a scale like “1 cm² represents 4 students”. Work out frequencies from area, not bar height.

14Probability

Year 1 + 2
Key points
  • Mutually exclusive: A and B can't both happen, so P(A ∩ B) = 0.
  • Independent: one happening doesn't affect the other, so P(A ∩ B) = P(A) × P(B).
  • Use Venn diagrams for overlapping events and tree diagrams for events in stages. Multiply along branches, add between outcomes.
  • P(A | B) is the probability of A given B has happened. On a Venn diagram, B becomes the whole space.
Formulas
  • Addition ruleP(A ∪ B) = P(A) + P(B) − P(A ∩ B)
  • ConditionalP(A|B)=P(A∩B)P(B)
  • Independence testP(A ∩ B) = P(A)P(B)  or  P(A | B) = P(A)
ExampleP(A) = 0.5, P(B) = 0.4 and P(A ∪ B) = 0.7. Find P(A ∩ B), decide whether A and B are independent, and find P(A | B).
Show solutionHide solution
  1. 0.7 = 0.5 + 0.4 − P(A ∩ B), so P(A ∩ B) = 0.2.
  2. P(A) × P(B) = 0.5 × 0.4 = 0.2 = P(A ∩ B), so they are independent.
  3. P(A | B) = 0.2 / 0.4 = 0.5, which equals P(A), as expected for independent events.
P(A ∩ B) = 0.2, independent, P(A | B) = 0.5
Exercise
  1. [2]A bag has 5 red and 3 blue counters. Two are taken without replacement. Find P(both red).
    Show answer

    5/14

    5/8 × 4/7 = 20/56.

  2. [2]Using the same bag, find P(one of each colour).
    Show answer

    15/28

    Red then blue or blue then red: 2 × (5/8 × 3/7) = 30/56.

Common mistake

Mutually exclusive and independent are different. Two events with non-zero probabilities can't be both: if they are mutually exclusive, knowing one happened tells you the other didn't.

15Binomial distribution

Year 1
Key points
  • X ~ B(n, p) counts successes in n trials.
  • Conditions: a fixed number of trials; two outcomes (success/failure); a constant probability p; independent trials.
  • Calculators give P(X = r) (binomial PD) and P(X ≤ r) (binomial CD). Convert other inequalities to ≤ first.
Formulas
  • ProbabilityP(X=r)=(nr)pr(1−p)n−r
  • Mean and varianceE(X) = npVar(X) = np(1 − p)
  • Converting inequalitiesP(X ≥ r) = 1 − P(X ≤ r − 1)P(X < r) = P(X ≤ r − 1)
ExampleX ~ B(12, 0.3). Find (a) P(X = 4) and (b) P(X ≥ 3).
Show solutionHide solution
  1. (a) P(X = 4) = 12C4 × 0.34 × 0.78 = 495 × 0.0081 × 0.057648 = 0.2311.
  2. (b) P(X ≥ 3) = 1 − P(X ≤ 2).
  3. P(X ≤ 2) = 0.0138 + 0.0712 + 0.1678 = 0.2528 (or binomial CD on a calculator).
(a) 0.2311  (b) 1 − 0.2528 = 0.7472
Exercise
  1. [2]X ~ B(20, 0.25). Find E(X) and Var(X).
    Show answer

    E(X) = 5, Var(X) = 3.75

    20 × 0.25 = 5; 5 × 0.75 = 3.75.

  2. [2]A fair coin is tossed 10 times. Find the probability of at most one head.
    Show answer

    11/1024 ≈ 0.0107

    P(0) + P(1) = 0.5¹⁰ + 10 × 0.5¹⁰ = 11 × 0.5¹⁰.

Common mistake

Read the inequality words carefully. “At least 3” is ≥ 3, “more than 3” is ≥ 4, “fewer than 3” is ≤ 2.

16Normal distribution

Year 2
Key points
  • X ~ N(μ, σ²) is a continuous, bell-shaped distribution, symmetrical about the mean μ. Points of inflection are at μ ± σ.
  • About 68% of data lies within 1σ of the mean, 95% within 2σ, and 99.7% within 3σ.
  • For a continuous distribution, P(X = a) = 0, so < and ≤ give the same answer.
  • Use the inverse normal function to find a value from a probability. To find unknown μ or σ, standardise and use z-values.
  • B(n, p) can be approximated by N(np, np(1 − p)) when n is large and p is close to 0.5. Apply a continuity correction.
Formulas
  • StandardisingZ=X−μσ,Z~N(0,12)
  • Continuity correctionP(X ≤ 7) → P(Y < 7.5)P(X < 7) → P(Y < 6.5)
ExampleHeights are modelled as X ~ N(170, 8²) cm. Find P(160 < X < 180).
Show solutionHide solution
  1. Standardise: (160 − 170)/8 = −1.25 and (180 − 170)/8 = 1.25.
  2. P(−1.25 < Z < 1.25) = 2Φ(1.25) − 1 by symmetry.
  3. = 2(0.89435) − 1 = 0.7887. (A calculator's normal CD gives this directly.)
0.7887 (4 d.p.)
Exercise
  1. [2]X ~ N(50, 4²). Find P(X > 56).
    Show answer

    0.0668

    z = 6/4 = 1.5; 1 − Φ(1.5) = 1 − 0.9332.

  2. [3]X ~ N(100, 15²). Find a such that P(X < a) = 0.9.
    Show answer

    a = 119.2 (1 d.p.)

    Φ⁻¹(0.9) = 1.2816, so a = 100 + 1.2816 × 15.

Common mistake

The second number in N(μ, σ²) is the variance. If a question says N(50, 16), then σ = 4, not 16.

17Hypothesis testing

Year 1 + 2
Key points
  • H₀ (null hypothesis) states the parameter's assumed value. H₁ (alternative) says how you think it has changed: <, > (one-tailed) or ≠ (two-tailed).
  • The significance level is the probability of rejecting H₀ when it is true. In a two-tailed test, split it equally between the two tails.
  • The critical region is the set of values that lead to rejecting H₀. The actual significance level is the true probability of landing in it.
  • Compare either the p-value with the significance level, or the test statistic with the critical region.
  • Tests you need: binomial proportion p, normal mean μ using X̄ ~ N(μ, σ²/n), and correlation using the PMCC.
Formulas
  • Sample meanX‾~N(μ,σ2n)
  • Test statisticz=x‾−μσ/n
  • Decisionp-value < significance level ⇒ reject H₀
ExampleA head teacher claims 30% of students walk to school. In a random sample of 20, only 2 walk. Test at the 5% level whether the proportion is lower than claimed.
Show solutionHide solution
  1. H₀: p = 0.3   H₁: p < 0.3 (one-tailed, 5%).
  2. Under H₀, X ~ B(20, 0.3).
  3. P(X ≤ 2) = 0.0355, which is less than 0.05.
  4. The result is significant, so reject H₀.
There is evidence that fewer than 30% of students walk to school.
Exercise
  1. [1]In a two-tailed test at the 10% level, what probability goes in each tail?
    Show answer

    5% in each tail

  2. [5]A sample of 16 from N(μ, 3²) has mean 51.8. Test H₀: μ = 50 against H₁: μ > 50 at the 5% level.
    Show answer

    z = 2.4 > 1.6449, so reject H₀. There is evidence the mean is greater than 50.

    X̄ ~ N(50, 9/16), so the standard error is 0.75 and z = 1.8 / 0.75 = 2.4. (p-value = 0.0082.)

Common mistake

Conclusions must be in context and non-definite. Write “there is evidence to suggest…”, not “this proves…”. Use the parameter (p or μ) in your hypotheses, not the sample value.
