Exam technique

Exam skills

Knowing the maths is half the job. This page covers what examiners are asking for, how marks are given, how to pace a paper, and the mistakes that cost students the most marks.

Pacing a paper

Most A Level Maths papers are 100 marks in 2 hours. Use the marks as a guide to time and to how much working is expected.

  • About 1.2 minutes per mark. A 5-mark question deserves about 6 minutes.
  • Leave 10 minutes at the end to check answers, units and accuracy.
  • Use the marks as a guide to working. 1 mark is usually one step or fact; 6 or more means several stages.

Command words

The first word of a question tells you what kind of answer gets full marks.

Show that
The answer is given, so the marks are for the working. Show every step clearly and end by writing the given result.Even if you can't do it, use the given answer in later parts.
Hence
You must use the previous part. Other methods score no marks.
Hence or otherwise
Using the previous part is usually quickest, but any correct method is allowed.
Prove
A complete logical argument from start to finish, with a concluding statement.
Exact
Leave answers in terms of surds, π, e, ln or fractions. No decimals.
Sketch
Show the shape, axis intercepts, turning points and asymptotes. It doesn't need to be to scale.
Find / Calculate / Determine
Work it out, showing enough working to justify the answer.
Write down / State
Little or no working needed. Often 1 mark.
Verify
Substitute the given values and show they work. Include a conclusion.
Interpret / Explain / Comment
Answer in words, in the context of the question (e.g. the units, the real situation).

How marks are awarded

Mark schemes use letters for each type of mark. Most marks are for method, so a wrong final answer can still score well if the working is shown.

M

Method marks: for a correct method applied to the problem, even with arithmetic slips.

A

Accuracy marks: for a correct answer, but only if the related M mark has been earned.

B

Independent marks: for a correct result or statement, regardless of method.

ft

Follow through: credit for using an earlier wrong answer correctly in a later part.

Mistakes that cost the most marks

Examiners' reports mention these year after year.

  1. Calculator in the wrong angle modeUse radians for calculus and whenever the interval is given in terms of π.
  2. Missing solutions to trig equationsSketch the graph and find every solution in the interval, not just the calculator's one.
  3. Forgetting + cEvery indefinite integral needs a constant, and it matters when solving differential equations.
  4. Not showing working on “show that” questionsJumping straight to the given answer scores nothing.
  5. Premature roundingKeep full calculator values until the final answer, then round to 3 s.f. unless told otherwise.
  6. Decimals when the question says “exact”Give √3/2, not 0.866, and ln 5, not 1.609.
  7. Mixing up σ and σ²N(50, 16) means a standard deviation of 4.
  8. Conclusions without contextA hypothesis test isn't finished until you say what the result means in the real situation.
  9. No force diagram in mechanicsIt takes 30 seconds and stops you missing a force or getting a sign wrong.
  10. Sign errors with bracketsTake extra care when subtracting a bracket and squaring negative numbers: (−3)² = 9, but −3² = −9.

How to revise effectively

Re-reading notes feels productive but doesn't stick. These methods are backed by research on learning.

Practise retrieval
Cover the solution and try each worked example yourself before checking. Struggling to recall is what makes it stick.
Space it out
Return to each topic several times over weeks, rather than once for hours.
Mix topics
In an exam, questions don't tell you the topic. Practise papers that mix topics so you learn to choose the method.
Keep an error log
Write down every mistake from practice papers and why you made it. Re-read it before each exam.
